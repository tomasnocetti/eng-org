# Engineering manifesto

How we expect engineers to behave, as things we aim for and patterns we do not accept. It
began as the management team's answer to "who do we want to work with" and later became our
promotion criteria.

## What we do

Every line of the manifesto is a behaviour a teammate can observe. Nothing in it says how to
write code. That lives in the code principles and the competency matrix.

We use it to decide promotions. A promotion case, for an engineer or for a manager, is judged on
the same four pillars, ownership, learning, collaboration and impact, and must include peer
feedback, because "impact must be visible beyond the manager". Career growth expectations derive
from it as well.

### The manifesto

> **What we stand for.** We believe great engineering is not just about writing code. It is
> about ownership, learning, and impact. Here, no one works alone. We support, mentor, and
> challenge each other to continuously improve. We build together and succeed together.
>
> **What we aim for** (what we work towards achieving every day)
>
> - **We take ownership.** We don't just complete tasks; we follow through, ensuring long-term success.
> - **We are curious learners.** We expand our knowledge, challenge ourselves, and elevate the team.
> - **We work as a team.** We collaborate, mentor, and communicate effectively to move forward together.
> - **We build for the future.** We find elegant solutions, write maintainable code, and anticipate problems before they arise.
> - **We move fast.** We prioritize and estimate effectively, iterate quickly, and balance speed with quality without compromising maintainability.
> - **We document our work.** We don't let valuable information live in silos; we make it accessible for the team.
> - **We act with humility and collaboration**, recognizing and valuing each other's contributions.
> - **We solve problems proactively.** We don't get blocked; we push forward, escalate when needed, and take initiative.
> - **We commit to the team.** We support, review, and challenge each other to do our best work, because trust is built through action.
> - **We communicate clearly.** We raise blockers early, keep the people who depend on us informed, and set expectations using the right channels and tools.
> - **We embrace feedback.** We reflect, iterate, and refine, learning from mistakes and successes alike.
>
> **What doesn't align with us** (patterns that hold us back)
>
> - **We don't avoid ownership.** Needing constant follow-ups or failing to follow through slows the team down.
> - **We don't work in isolation.** If you don't ask for help or share knowledge, you limit what we can achieve together.
> - **We don't resist feedback.** When areas for improvement are pointed out, they should be addressed, not dismissed.
> - **We don't skip or dismiss communication.** Not raising blockers, missing context, or keeping information to yourself slows execution.
> - **We don't cut corners.** Rushed PRs, ignoring engineering standards, or skipping reviews erode long-term success.
> - **We don't hide knowledge.** Keeping critical insights undocumented makes it harder for everyone to grow.
>
> **Final thoughts.** We are problem-solvers, not just coders. We care about efficiency,
> impact, and collaboration. We embrace continuous learning, mentorship, and responsibility.
> This is what defines us as engineers.

## Why

We wrote it because our expectations of each other were implicit. We had a competency matrix,
company values and an onboarding document, and none of them said how we expected engineers to
behave with their teammates. New hires learned it slowly, by bumping into it. The management
team framed the problem with Ben Horowitz's distinction: explicit culture is what you say,
implicit culture is what you do, and we only had the second.

A first attempt had already failed. An offsite produced five slogans, "code without fear", "tech
debt works as a credit card" and the like. Nothing shipped for eleven months. The slogans were
memorable and empty. None of them tells a new engineer what to do on Tuesday.

What shipped came from describing real people. Two managers wrote independently, one from the
teammate's seat and one from the manager's, answering three questions about engineers we had
actually worked with: what do we love, what do we expect, what don't we want. The manifesto is
the merge of those lists, and almost every line traces back to one. "Needing constant
follow-ups" was the manager's first item under what we don't want. We kept the negatives because
they were the sharpest answers: positive values are things everyone agrees with, while the
"don'ts" name the behaviours that have cost us, in words a manager can point at. The positives
are aims, not a baseline; a reviewer put it as "there's a process to get here". The
anti-patterns are the floor.

We cut the first draft hard. What went was every technical item, which the competency matrix
already covered level by level, and every effort judgment, which is feedback for a one-on-one
rather than a principle the org signs up to. A request to add a line on security was deferred as
"not a base requirement today".

Ten months after publication, the promotion policies for engineers and for managers adopted its
four pillars as what a promotion recognizes, and required peer feedback for the same reason the
manifesto says trust is built through action: if the behaviours are real, the team has already
seen them. That reuse is the strongest evidence we have that the manifesto is used.

## What we learned

Describe people, then generalize. The version that shipped came from two managers listing what
they loved, expected and refused in engineers they had actually worked with. The version that
came from a culture brainstorm produced slogans and sat for a year. If we did it again we would
skip the brainstorm and start with the three questions.

Leave skill out of it. Most of what we cut from the first draft was anything the competency
matrix already covered. A manifesto that restates the levels competes with the matrix and loses;
one that only covers conduct has no competitor.

We have not measured anything. We do not know whether onboarding got faster or whether hiring
changed. What we can see is that the document was reused unchanged as promotion criteria, which
a document nobody believed in would not have been.

This worked at roughly 15 to 25 engineers across five product teams and two guilds, written by a
management team small enough that each author could picture every engineer. At a larger org the
exercise still works, but the answers would need to come from more than two people.
