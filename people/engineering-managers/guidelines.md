# Engineering Manager guidelines

Source document, anonymized. Written 2024-09 by the first engineer to go through the
transition together with their manager, reviewed by the other managers, last revised
2025-08. See [Engineering managers](../engineering-managers.md) for how it is used.

## How do you add value?

As a manager, your work is accomplished through the success of your team. Your productivity
is not measured by daily outputs but by how effectively your team meets milestones and
delivers on commitments. Your success is an aggregation of the team's success, and your
timeline for feeling productive shifts from daily to weekly or even monthly.

Remember, your role is to leverage the power of the team to make things happen. If the team
doesn't operate efficiently without your direct involvement, something is off. While your
presence should act as a catalyst for things to happen faster, the team should still
function smoothly in your absence. Empower your team to own their responsibilities and
foster a culture of autonomy and trust.

Managers are enablers who make sure team members have what they need to succeed while also
ensuring the bigger picture is not lost in day-to-day activities. You create value by
identifying areas for improvement, delegating responsibilities effectively, and supporting
team growth and innovation.

### Requirements for the position

- Strong product sense
- Ability to manage cross-functional initiatives that sometimes span beyond engineering
- Familiarity with the functional areas of your team
- Excellent written and verbal communication skills
- Any additional experience required by the position's specialty
- You share our mission and values and work in accordance with them
- Product company experience
- Startup experience
- Computer science education or equivalent experience
- **Soft skills:** demonstrated emotional intelligence, adaptability, conflict resolution
  skills, and the ability to build strong cross-functional relationships
- **Proactivity:** ability to align personal goals of engineers with team and company goals

## Responsibilities of an Engineering Manager

- **Push back when necessary.** Identify when to push back on product or technical decisions
  that could lead to long-term risks. This includes ensuring that your team does not
  overcommit to unrealistic timelines, pointing out when additional resources are needed,
  and flagging critical risks before they become blockers.
- **Manage tech debt proactively.** Maintain awareness of the team's technical debt and
  communicate the importance of addressing it. Create plans to reduce tech debt and ensure
  it's not neglected in favor of short-term gains. Advocate for time in planning to clean up
  tech debt or refactor when needed. It is suggested that there's at least 20% of tech debt
  each cycle.
- **Make critical decisions on tech debt.** Be pragmatic about tech debt. Know when to incur
  it to move quickly and test new functionality, and when to invest in thoughtful design
  from the start. Balance speed with sustainability by weighing trade-offs and long-term
  impact.
- **Manage a multidisciplinary team of engineers** and help them grow their skills and
  experience through regular feedback and mentorship.
- **Oversee project plans** and ensure alignment between stakeholders and the team.
- **Provide technical guidance and coaching**, while ensuring technical decisions align with
  long-term goals.
- **Maintain empathy for your team** by staying connected to workflows, conducting code
  reviews, and working on non-critical-path tasks.
- **Foster psychological safety** and support team well-being by maintaining open
  communication and trust.
- **Prevent single points of failure.** It's essential to ensure that no team member,
  including yourself, becomes a single point of failure. The team should continue to operate
  smoothly, even if a key person is unavailable. This can be achieved by:
  - Encouraging knowledge sharing through documentation, pair programming, and regular
    hand-offs.
  - Ensuring tasks are well-distributed and that multiple team members are familiar with
    critical areas of the system.
  - Actively identifying and addressing knowledge silos within the team.
  - Developing a system for clear ownership and backup for every critical function or
    domain.

## Dos and don'ts

- **Do promote knowledge sharing.** Always encourage the team to document key work and share
  knowledge openly. This ensures that no single person is indispensable and that others can
  step in when necessary.
- **Don't let knowledge silo.** Avoid situations where only one person has expertise on a
  critical system or component. Proactively ensure there is redundancy in knowledge across
  the team.
- **Do push back on unrealistic timelines or risky decisions.** Part of your responsibility
  is ensuring the team's success, which includes knowing when to challenge expectations or
  demands that may overburden the team or introduce significant risks. Always flag potential
  issues, such as unrealistic deadlines or scope creep.
- **Don't let tech debt accumulate unchecked.** Tech debt can slow teams down over time.
  Advocate for addressing it at the right time to avoid compromising long-term progress.
- **Do be critical about when to incur tech debt.** Know when it's necessary to build
  quickly and test something in the market, but also be vigilant about which areas of the
  system require more thought upfront to avoid long-term complications.
- **Do focus on where you add the most value.** Avoid spending too much time on tasks that
  can be handled by the team. Instead, focus on removing blockers, making strategic
  decisions, and supporting your team in delivering at a high level.
- **Do regularly provide feedback and guidance to your reports.** Developing your team is a
  crucial responsibility of an engineering manager. Feedback and guidance should be shared
  consistently on a weekly basis. If you notice an area where one of your direct reports can
  improve, address it as soon as possible. Consider using quick impromptu meetings to
  facilitate this discussion.
- **Don't wait for 1:1 meetings or performance reviews to give feedback.** Feedback should
  be shared constantly, as soon as you identify areas for improvement.
- **Do set a positive example of work-life balance.** It's important for everyone on your
  team to take occasional breaks or time off to recharge. It's crucial that you also
  disconnect and take time for yourself to demonstrate this balance.
- **Don't take on critical-path work.** While you should remain hands-on to some extent,
  avoid taking on work that is critical to release timelines, as regular managerial duties
  may pull you away and cause delays.
- **Plan to review more than you write.** Focus your technical contributions on code reviews
  rather than writing new code. Reviews allow you to stay informed about the team's work and
  provide valuable feedback, without becoming a bottleneck.
- **Keep yourself hands-on without becoming a blocker.** Being a manager doesn't mean you
  can't code anymore. Stay engaged through code reviews, pair programming, and tackling
  non-blocking tasks like bugs or small improvements.
- **Product Managers are your partner in crime.** Collaborate closely with Product Managers
  to ensure alignment on priorities, roadmaps, and team focus. We expect every engineer to
  be a product ambassador, working together to deliver meaningful results.

### Why we focus on technical Engineering Managers

We believe that Engineering Managers who are technically skilled and understand the work
their team is doing can provide stronger leadership. Your credibility as an Engineering
Manager increases significantly when the team believes that you could do their job if
needed. While you won't be coding full-time, maintaining a level of technical expertise
allows you to:

- **Earn trust and credibility.** When your team knows that you understand the technical
  challenges they face, they are more likely to trust your guidance and decision-making.
- **Provide meaningful feedback.** Hands-on technical experience allows you to give more
  effective feedback during code reviews and design discussions, technically sound and
  aligned with the business objectives.
- **Make informed trade-offs.** You are better equipped to balance speed and quality,
  understanding when it's appropriate to incur technical debt to move faster and when it's
  important to invest time in crafting a robust solution. You'll also be better positioned
  to communicate these trade-offs to stakeholders.
- **Stay connected to the team's challenges.** You will be able to recognize when certain
  tasks are more complex than they appear and adjust planning and expectations accordingly.

## Performance indicators

Key indicators, tailored to a weekly planning approach.

1. **Team productivity.**
   - Weekly planning completion: how well the team adheres to the priorities set during
     weekly planning.
   - Cycle time: the time it takes for tasks to move from "in progress" to "done".
   - Task throughput: how many tasks the team completes per week.
2. **Team health and engagement.**
   - Team morale and engagement: use regular 1:1s and surveys to assess satisfaction and
     motivation.
   - Workload balance: track whether the same individuals are overburdened or underutilized.
3. **Code quality and technical debt.**
   - Tech debt management: how often tech debt tasks are planned and completed.
   - Defect rate: the number of bugs or defects found. High rates may indicate that the team
     is moving too quickly or needs better QA processes.
4. **Collaboration and communication.**
   - Cross-team dependencies are accounted for in weekly planning and cross-team projects
     stay on track.
5. **Delivery and outcomes.**
   - On-time delivery for high-priority tasks. Frequent delays might require rethinking how
     tasks are prioritized.
6. **Continuous improvement and learning.**
   - Retrospective action items are incorporated into weekly planning and tracked.
7. **Personal performance as an EM.**
   - Team retention and satisfaction: feedback from 1:1s and upward feedback.
   - Stakeholder satisfaction: regular feedback from stakeholders.
8. **Risk management.**
   - Risks are surfaced during weekly planning and mitigated before they become critical.

## Onboarding plan

### First month

When onboarding as an Engineering Manager, you'll typically join an existing team or be part
of a new team forming from scratch. During the first four weeks, your focus will be on
contributing as a regular engineer to learn the ropes and build credibility with the team.
This period allows you to understand how things work and how you can contribute to
improvements without immediately taking on the EM responsibilities.

Goals for this phase include understanding:

- Team personalities and dynamics
- Team responsibilities and domain expertise
- Team interactions and communication flows
- Local development setup and processes
- Core technologies and key vendors
- High-level architecture

Look for improvement opportunities during this time, but make sure you understand the
rationale behind existing decisions before suggesting changes. Gather context first, then
make data-driven suggestions.

### After 30-60 days: leveling up as a manager

By the end of the first 30-60 days, you should be transitioning to full managerial
responsibilities. During this time, you should:

- Own the engineering meetings for the team.
- Be identified as the primary point of contact for the team.
- Contribute to the codebase and delegate tasks based on complexity and the seniority of
  team members.
- Provide thoughtful input on product improvements based on conversations with different
  stakeholders.
- Familiarize yourself with product integrations, including key vendor relationships.
- Start offering feedback on team processes and propose alternative solutions when
  appropriate.
- Address and resolve production issues with the support of your team.

### End of 60-90 days: driving outcomes autonomously

By the 60-90 day mark, you should feel confident leading your team and driving outcomes
independently:

- Have a deep understanding of the team's domain and effectively communicate technical
  concepts with any team member.
- Proactively identify blockers or productivity challenges and find solutions to remove
  them.
- Lead the team in fixing production issues with minimal support.
- Instill confidence in your team, earning their trust through strong communication and
  problem-solving abilities.

## Onboarding checklist

- [ ] Schedule 1:1s with all the members of your team to understand their career goals and
      get to know them personally.
- [ ] Set up 1:1s with other managers, especially those whose teams collaborate closely with
      yours.
- [ ] Gain a deep understanding of your team's scope of work. Clearly define boundaries
      between teams to ensure smooth collaboration.
- [ ] Actively review PRs from your team to gain context on ongoing work and ensure
      alignment with team goals.
- [ ] Roll up your sleeves. Find some minor bugs within your team so that you can get
      familiarized with the codebase.
