# Engineering managers

When what we expect from a manager is written nowhere, each new manager brings their own
version of the job, and a team's culture and dynamics shift with whoever leads it. We wrote
the role down so that reports and senior staff can both point to it, and we grow managers
from inside through a phased transition in which the candidate writes the plan, takes over
process before people, and is confirmed only once the team already runs under them.

## What we do

### The role, in writing

A small set of documents carries the role and the path into it. The full text of each is
kept next to this entry:

| Document | Answers |
| -------- | ------- |
| [Position description](engineering-managers/position-description.md) | What the role is, and what it is not |
| [Guidelines](engineering-managers/guidelines.md) | How to operate as a manager once you are one |
| [Promotion policy](engineering-managers/promotion-policy.md) and its [nomination template](engineering-managers/promotion-nomination-template.md) | The bar for confirming the title |
| [Transition playbook](engineering-managers/transition-playbook.md) | The sequence to get someone there |
| [Growth plan template](engineering-managers/growth-plan-template.md) | The plan a manager writes with each report |

The position description says what the role is and is not:

> **Mission.** Enable the success, performance and well-being of the team. Help the team
> deliver meaningful outcomes by creating the right environment for autonomy, alignment and
> growth, balancing day-to-day execution with long-term health.
>
> **Responsibilities.** Grow people. Own execution with product and design. Ensure the
> quality of the technical implementation. Keep the team environment healthy. Make pragmatic
> trade-offs between tech debt, velocity and quality. Prevent bottlenecks, so the team does
> not depend on a single person, including yourself.

It also draws three boundaries, which we read this way:

- **Technical, as a calibrator.** We expect a manager to be technical. Their part is to
  calibrate and align the team's technical decisions, while deep technical direction is
  driven together with staff engineers and senior team members.
- **Coding, but not as the primary task.** We expect a manager to code. It is not where most
  of their time goes, and it stays off the critical path: reviews, guidance, bugs and small
  improvements.
- **Not a solo role.** Team and product success come through collaboration across
  disciplines.

The guidelines say how to operate once you are one. The rules that do the most work:

- Your productivity is the team's. The timeline for feeling productive moves from daily to
  weekly or monthly. If the team does not run without your direct involvement, something is
  off.
- Stay technical. Review more than you write. Take bugs and small improvements, never
  critical-path work, because management duties will pull you away and delay the release.
- Give feedback weekly and as soon as you see something. Do not save it for a 1:1 or a
  performance review.
- Push back on unrealistic timelines and risky decisions, and flag risks before they block.
- Plan tech debt into every cycle. We suggest at least 20%.
- No single point of failure in people or systems. Every critical function has an owner and
  a backup.
- The product manager is your partner. Align on priorities and roadmap together.
- Take time off and disconnect, so the team sees that it can.

The promotion policy sets the bar for confirming the title and is covered in
[Promotions](promotions.md): at least six months acting as a direct manager, upward feedback
from the team, and a written nomination.

We measure a manager through the team, not through the manager: delivery velocity, errors
and bugs, engagement with the product, and how the team feels.

### The transition

An engineer who wants to manage goes through this sequence with their own manager. Roughly
three months to functional ownership of the team and twelve to a settled manager.

| Window | Phase | What happens |
| ------ | ----- | ------------ |
| Day 0 | Intent and contract | The candidate drafts the plan: first steps, goals and a timeline. Their manager adds to it, sets the weekly 1:1, writes down the role and the team's boundaries, and explains the career path beyond the role. |
| Weeks 1–4 | Embed and diagnose | Context homework with a checkpoint date: the product manager's role, team dynamics, the team's real trade-offs. They join the conversations and bring ideas. Ownership stays small and reversible. |
| Weeks 4–8 | Rituals and process | They own standups and planning, a quality ritual before significant releases, RFC discipline, and written procedures for recurring operational work. Responsibilities move over one at a time. |
| Weeks 8–12 | People and stakeholders | 1:1s with each report, with the product manager and with peer managers. The stakeholder relationship is written down with its mechanics. |
| Week 13 | Transition retro | A structured session covering the candidate, the process and the manager running it. |
| Week 14 | They write the role | A tracked deliverable: a contribution to the guidelines and a written definition of their team's scope. |
| Months 3–12 | Calibration | Quarterly focus items they author, a recurring feedback session, growth plans and performance reviews for their reports, incidents and post-mortems as a management skill. |

The plan belongs to the candidate. When an engineer tells us they want to become a manager,
we do not give them a ready-made program to follow. We ask them to come back with their own
first steps, goals and timeline, and their manager then completes it with them. As a rule of
thumb the goals are about 70% the candidate's and 30% the manager's, with dates on both
sides, so the candidate knows what to expect and when.

The retro uses a fixed set of questions:

1. What did we do well in the process? What could have been improved?
2. Were the expectations clear?
3. How was the transition from doing the work to managing it?
4. What are your strengths and weaknesses in the role so far?
5. What is still missing: on your side, on the team's side, and on mine?
6. What action items come out of this?

The recurring feedback session during calibration also uses fixed questions, asked the same
way each time:

1. What are your priorities today?
2. What are the team's priorities today?
3. What are you doing to support those priorities?
4. How are you technically supporting the team?
5. Do you have enough context on what the team is doing, its challenges and its timelines?

When something is off in the team, we coach a loop and not an answer: step outside the team
and look at it critically, gather enough context to make the call, tell product and
stakeholders in writing even when the news is bad, and drive the action items. Feedback to
the candidate is given the same day, in writing, as a perception to test in the next 1:1,
with a concrete alternative attached.

The transition is complete when the artifacts exist, not when a date passes: the role and
boundaries, the candidate's plan, the context notes, the retro, the contribution to the
guidelines, the quarterly focus items, a growth plan for each report, and a handoff document
for their own time off that names who owns what while they are away.

We read readiness from the team. It keeps planning and keeps stakeholders informed while the
manager is on leave. Bug volume trends down. Bad news reaches product before product asks.
The manager produces growth plans and promotion proposals for their people, and pushes back
on scope and timelines without escalating.

## Why

We had new managers who did not align with our culture, and nothing written to hold them or
ourselves to. The culture and dynamics of some teams shifted as a result. That is when we
decided to put on paper what we expect from a manager, in a form that the people being
managed and the senior staff could both refer to.

We grow managers from inside because a person who has been here carries the culture of the
company with them. There is a second benefit that is harder to count: the commitment that
comes from investing in someone's growth, on both sides. Few things in this job are better
than watching someone grow into it.

The candidate drafts the plan because owning the path is the first test of the role: a
manager is someone who works out what needs to happen without being handed the steps. For
the same reason they write part of the role itself. Putting the job into words shows a new
manager where their understanding has gaps, and it leaves a document the next one starts
from.

We want managers who stay technical because a team trusts guidance from someone who could do
their job, and because trade-offs between speed and quality cannot be made or explained from
a distance.

## What we learned

### What worked

We have run the transition successfully with three managers.

Process before people. A candidate who first owns the ceremonies, the quality ritual and the
RFC habit builds credibility with the team before holding anyone's career in their hands,
and the team learns what is next even when the manager is absent.

Writing as the coaching device. Asking for a written report on the team against an explicit
list of questions, with a review date, teaches more than giving the answers.

Time off as the test. A handoff document that lets the team run while the manager is away
is better proof of readiness than any review.

Measuring the team and not the manager kept the incentive where the role says it should be.

### What could be improved

Protecting a new manager too much limits their exposure. They need to meet real problems
while someone is still shadowing them, because that is when there is room to debate how to
solve them. How much to expose and how much to shield is a trade-off, and calibrating it
takes time. The working rule is that the new manager brings the problems and the senior one
supports.

Expectations need dates. A new manager should know what is expected of them and when, and
what they can expect in return. Goals written together, mostly by the candidate, are the
place to make that explicit.

Delegation needs trust, and the accountability does not move with it. Hand over real
problems so the new manager can experiment, and remain responsible for the team while they
do. That balance stays difficult.

Write the role and the boundaries with neighbouring teams on day zero. Left for later, the
ambiguity comes back long after the transition is over. The same goes for what lies above
the role: a new manager will ask what they are working toward, and needs an answer.

Becoming the bottleneck is not a lesson learned once. It returns in different forms:
centralizing the team's communication, covering for a blocked engineer, absorbing the
team's interruptions to protect it. Each looks like care and behaves like a dependency.
