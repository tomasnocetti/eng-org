# Promotions

Without a written case, promotions are decided by whoever heads the area, and once there are
several managers nobody can see what a promotion requires or compare two cases. So the manager
makes the case in writing, in a fixed nomination format, and the case has to include what peers
say. A promotion recognizes that someone is already operating at the next level.

## What we do

A promotion starts with a sponsor. Nobody is promoted without a manager willing to write the
case and drive it forward. The sponsor writes the nomination in a fixed format, one template for
engineers and a longer one for engineering managers.

The case is judged on four things, in this order. Impact comes first: sustained outcomes for
engineering and for the business, visible beyond the manager. Then competencies: the candidate
consistently shows most of the next level's expectations in the competency matrix. Then
criticality to the org, which counts in favour but never substitutes for the first two. Peer
feedback is mandatory. If collaborators do not already describe the person as operating at the
next level, the case is not ready.

For engineering managers the bar adds three things. At least six months as a direct manager.
Upward feedback from their reports, and no unresolved interpersonal or management issues. And a
section on growth opportunities, even when the candidate is ready, so the promotion comes with
the next development conversation already written.

Every nomination for a cycle then goes into a shared space that all engineering managers can
read. Managers discuss the cases and flag concerns before anything is decided. For an engineer,
sign-off rests with the engineering manager after that discussion. For an engineering manager,
the heads of the area sit down and decide together.

Compensation is decided separately. Performance reviews run every six months, and the People
team walks each manager through the proposed outcome, where the manager can agree or push back.
The final call on money sits with the People team and Finance, not with the nominating manager.

### The nomination template for engineers

> **Candidate.** Name, current level, proposed level, manager, date.
>
> **1. Skills and competencies.** Does the engineer consistently demonstrate next-level
> behaviours from the competency matrix? Examples of next-level skills, 3 to 5 bullets.
>
> **2. Impact, engineering and business.** Describe the meaningful, sustained impact the
> engineer has created. Impact highlights, 3 to 6 bullets. These may include: delivery of
> important outcomes; improving system quality, architecture or operations; accelerating
> execution or unblocking teams; increasing reliability or reducing risk; improving customer
> experience or product value; reducing costs or manual effort; supporting, mentoring or
> raising the level of others.
>
> **3. Peer and cross-team feedback.** Short comments or summaries from collaborators who have
> worked with the engineer.
>
> **4. Criticality.** How is this person providing unique leverage to the organization? 1 to 2
> bullets.
>
> **5. Manager recommendation.** A clear, brief statement summarizing why the engineer is
> already operating at the next level.

The manager template has the same spine with two additions: feedback is gathered from direct
reports, cross-functional partners and other engineering leaders rather than from peers alone,
and the manager must name 2 to 4 growth opportunities before making the recommendation.

## Why

Before this there was no promotion process. Performance reviews ran company-wide twice a year,
so every manager knew where each of their engineers was strong and where they needed to grow.
What happened with that knowledge was open. Promotions were driven case by case by the heads of
the area, on their judgment and their initiative. With one or two heads and a dozen engineers
that works. With several managers and several teams it does not: engineers could not see what a
promotion required, and managers had no common way to make or compare a case.

The template was the first step, and it was deliberately just a template. We did not want a
committee or a calibration bureaucracy, but a promotion deserved more than one person's opinion.
So the second step was consensus without ceremony: nominations go into a shared space, every
manager reads them, and anyone can flag a concern before the decision. The sponsor rule comes
from the same place. A case only moves if a manager is willing to put their name on it.

The policy states the principle in one line: a promotion is not a reward or a tenure marker, it
confirms what is already visible to the team. If that is true, the sponsor should be able to
write it down in five sections, and other people should already be saying it. Hence the two
rules that shape the template: impact first, and peer feedback required. Both came out of
review. A senior manager asked that impact be moved to the top, and it was. The same reviewer
asked that peer feedback draw on the written review-tool feedback for the candidate, not only on
hallway summaries.

Criticality is listed last and labelled a supporting factor on purpose. Being the only person
who understands a system is a reason to fix the system, not a reason to promote the person. It
counts when it comes with the skills and the impact, and not otherwise.

The manager bar is stricter because the evidence is different. An engineer's impact is in the
work; a manager's is in the team, so the policy asks whether the team delivers without them,
whether reports are growing, and what reports say upward. Six months as a direct manager gives a
team record to look at. The growth-opportunities section is required even for a ready candidate
because the promotion is the start of a role, not the end of one.

Two points are still open from review. The competency bar reads "most of the expectations of the
next level", and a reviewer asked to quantify it, for example as three quarters of matrix items
at the next level. We have not, and cases are argued rather than counted. The same reviewer
asked that past cycle outcomes, review scores and prior increases, be part of the case. They are
not in the template yet.

Promotions are one of the places the [engineering
manifesto](../principles/engineering-manifesto.md) does work: the four pillars the policy names
as what a promotion recognizes are the manifesto's.

## What we learned

Two things came out of it that we did not set out to get. Engineers now know what a promotion
requires, because the criteria and the template are written down and the same for everyone. That
clarity was the main effect. And the nominations turned out to be worth reading in their own
right: a written case is a compact account of what someone did and how they got there, and
sharing it with the other managers spreads that knowledge in a way a score never did.

We have not measured anything beyond that. We do not know whether the same people would have
been promoted under the old pattern.

The unresolved question is whether the bar stays qualitative. "Most of the expectations" lets a
sponsor argue a case; a percentage would let a sponsor count one. So far we argue. The request
to quantify came from someone who has to compare cases across teams, and that pressure grows
with the number of managers, which is the same force that made the old pattern stop working.
